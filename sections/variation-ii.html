11 Vowel Shifts in North American English: Northern Cities Shift

The Northern Cities Shift (NCS) affects Canadian and major northern U.S. cities, including Syracuse, Rochester, Buffalo, Cleveland, Akron, Detroit, Chicago, Milwaukee, and Madison.

12 Negative Concord

Languages vary in how they mark negation in a sentence. While some languages, like English, require a single negation, others, like French, require multiple elements to express a single negated meaning—this phenomenon is called negative concord.

12.1 English (Single Negation)

  1. He saw nobody.
  2. He didn’t see anyone.

English uses a single negative marker (e.g., “nobody” or “not”) per clause. Using more than one can result in a double negative, which is ungrammatical in Standard English.

12.2 Spanish (Negative Concord)

Spanish requires both the sentential negator no and a negative word like nada (“nothing”) to form a grammatical negative sentence.

  1.  

    No hay nada de comer

    Neg there.is nothing of eat.inf

    “There is nothing to eat.”

  1.  

    Juan no comió nada

    Juan neg ate nothing

    “Juan didn’t eat anything.”

12.2.1 Ungrammatical Sentences

  1.  

    Hay nada de comer

    there.is nothing of eat.inf

    “Intended: There is nothing to eat”

  1.  

    Juan comió nada

    Juan ate nothing

    “Intended: Juan ate nothing”

12.4 Appalachian English (Negative Concord)

Appalachian English — like many dialects of English — allows and often requires multiple negative elements in a sentence. These do not cancel each other out, but instead work together to express a single negation.

  1. Nobody didn’t see him.
  2. Nobody couldn’t handle him.
  3. Nothin’ hadn’t come up.
  4. Nobody else won’t move in it, I know I ain’t.
  5. Nobody wouldn’t say nothin’ about it.
  6. Or at least they never said nothing.
  7. I don’t know nothing about that.
    ‘I don’t know anything about that.’
  8. It’s some question that should never leave nobody’s mind.
    ‘It’s a question that should never leave anybody’s mind.’
  9. But we never did take any milk and butter to town.

In these examples, multiple negatives like nobody, nothing, didn’t, ain’t, won’t, and couldn’t all co-occur in one clause to reinforce a single negative meaning. This pattern parallels negative concord systems found in languages like Spanish or Turkish.

In contrast to Standard English, these sentences are grammatical in Appalachian English and are widely used by native speakers of this dialect.

13 The Northern Subject Rule

In Standard American English and Standard British English, when a present tense verb co-occurs with a third person singular subject, the verb appears with an -s suffix, as in (1). In all other cases, it lacks the suffix, as in (2):

  1. The cat / it sits on the mat.
  2. I / you / we / the cats / they sit on the mat.

However, many varieties of English do not follow this distribution. Some follow a pattern known as the Northern Subject Rule (NSR).

Beginning with Old Northumbrian (a dialect of Old English), certain northern English and Scottish dialects developed a distinct pattern of subject–verb agreement. Due to colonization and migration, similar patterns are also found in varieties spoken in Ireland and Appalachia.
